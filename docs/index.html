DeadlineFloat

Your deadlines, always in view — a twelve-point bar down the edge of your Mac.

↓ Scroll
At rest

Twelve points wide

Down the edge of every display: the whole day as a ruler, midnight to midnight, every event drawn as long as it actually lasts, and a needle for now. Beside it floats what is on — or next — with a live countdown, so you can read it without opening anything at all.

It never steals focus from whatever you are typing in, and there is no Dock icon. Just an hourglass in the menu bar.

Open

It stretches, it doesn’t fade

Rest the pointer on the sliver and it widens sideways — the very same blocks growing into today's column while the task list arrives beside them. Deadlines on the left, the day's schedule on the right.

“HAPPENING NOW · 1 hr 13 min left” while you are inside something. “DUE NEXT · in 2 hr 14 min” before the next thing lands. Whole minutes, in words, never seconds — and always truncated, never rounded up, because a countdown that overstates the time you have left is the one way it could actually hurt you.

The week

A day, or all seven

One day, two, three, or the week. The bar widens a step each time and the calendar adds a column; the list grows a section for every day it can now see, and all-day items ride above the columns.

Every block is the colour Google Calendar actually shows you — per event or per calendar, the palette Google draws with today, not the decade-old hexes its API still reports. Urgency is carried by the countdown's wording and colour instead, so the colour you chose for a class stays that class's colour.

Done

Swipe it done

A two-finger swipe marks a deadline done, with a tap from the trackpad. Finished items wait in a drawer above the list — scroll up past the barrier to see them, and swipe one back whenever you like.

What counts as a deadline is yours: titles that start with DUE or SUBMIT, or contain due or deadline. Every keyword is editable, matching is whole-word, and DONE or CANCELLED items always stay hidden.

The rest of it

Everything else worth knowing

Native Swift and SwiftUI — no Electron. Liquid Glass on macOS 26 and later, with a hand-built glass fallback down to macOS 14.

Correct at the edges

Daylight saving, midnight versus noon, recurring series, multi-day all-day events, duplicates across calendars, going offline, and Google's rate limits — all handled, all covered by tests.

Drag to reschedule

Take hold of a block and it moves — up and down for a different time, sideways for a different day. Take hold of an edge and only that edge moves. It snaps to five minutes, ⌥ gets you the minute, and the new time goes back to Google the moment you let go. Off until you ask for it, because it is the one thing that needs more than read access.

Works offline

The last sync is cached inside the app's own sandbox container, so the window still tells you what is due on a train with no signal. It refreshes every five minutes, on wake from sleep, and whenever the day or the time zone changes underneath it.

Accessible by design

SF Pro throughout, every inset on one spacing grid, contrast checked in both appearances, and Reduce Transparency, Increase Contrast and Reduce Motion all honoured. Text size scales the whole panel, not just the titles.

What it can see

One scope. Two hosts. No server.

DeadlineFloat has no backend to send anything to. What it may ask Google for is not a promise in a policy — it is enforced in the code that makes the request.

  • One Google scope: calendar.readonly. No profile, no email address, no other service.
  • Read-only by construction. Every Calendar API request must be a GET, enforced in code, so the app cannot create, change or delete an event even if it wanted to.
  • Dragging events is opt-in. Turn on Moving events and the app asks for calendar.events as well — and still permits itself exactly one write: a PATCH of one event's start and end. Never a create, never a delete, never a calendar.
  • Two reachable hosts, both Google's. Every other host is refused before the request leaves your Mac.
  • Tokens live in the macOS Keychain. Calendar data is cached only inside the app's own sandbox container, so the window works offline.
  • No analytics, no telemetry, no crash reporting, no server. Nothing about you is collected, because there is nowhere for it to go.

Installing it — four steps, no account

  1. Open the disk image and drag DeadlineFloat into Applications. Launch at Login only works from there.
  2. Open it. A sliver appears down the edge of each display and an hourglass appears in the menu bar.
  3. Press Sign in with Google. Your browser does the signing in — DeadlineFloat never sees your password.
  4. Google asks to allow one thing: see and download any calendar you can access. That is the whole request, unless you later turn on Moving events.

While the app is in Google's review queue you will see a “Google hasn't verified this app” notice on first sign-in. Choose Advanced → Go to DeadlineFloat to continue; the permission being granted is the read-only one above, whatever the notice says.
